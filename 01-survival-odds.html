Survival Odds

The camp's language model doesn't read minds. It continues your text with what is most likely to come next. Every word you send moves the odds toward some answers and away from others. Out here, a vague question gets a vague answer, and vague answers get people hurt. Prompt craft is the skill of moving the odds toward the answer you actually need.

Drill 1

Watch the odds move

The east-side scouting party is back with a long, rambling patrol log. Reyes, the camp leader, pastes it into the camp AI and types one line. What she needs is a five-bullet brief she can act on before dark. Switch on each piece of context and watch how much of the probability lands on that brief.

The prompt
6%chance Reyes gets the brief she needs

Illustrative numbers, built to show the direction and rough size of each effect. Real models put probability on individual tokens; these six outcomes stand in for families of possible responses.

Each dot is one run of the same prompt. Orange means Reyes got her brief.

Three things to notice. First, the bare prompt isn't wrong; it's underdetermined, so the odds spread across every reasonable reading of "summarize." Second, the role lever barely helps and pulls some odds toward a street-by-street tactical dump, because a role describes the writer and says nothing about the reader. Third, even a strong prompt is still a roll of the dice. Run it 20 times and you may see a miss. Low temperature sharpens the odds; high temperature flattens them.

How the machine thinks

Every answer is a chain of bets

The model writes one token at a time. Each token is drawn from a distribution conditioned on everything before it: your prompt, plus whatever it has written so far. The odds of a whole response are the product of those steps.

P(response | prompt) = ∏ P(tokeni | prompt, token1 … tokeni−1)

You only control one part of that condition: the prompt. Two rules of survival follow.

The first steps decide the route. Once a response heads off as a three-paragraph essay, every later token follows from that start, like a patrol that took the wrong turn out of the gate. Steer the opening and you steer the whole thing.

Silence gets filled with the typical case. Whatever you leave unsaid, the model resolves toward what is most common in text that looks like yours. If your situation isn't the typical one (and in an apocalypse, it rarely is), you have to say so.

Drill 2

Inspect your own prompt

Paste a prompt you've sent recently. The inspector looks for signs of each lever. It reads wording only, so treat it as a gear check, not a grade.

    Know its limits

    What prompting can't do

    Create knowledge

    If the model doesn't know the Fifth Street bridge collapsed and you don't tell it, no phrasing makes the right route likely. Put the scout report in the prompt.

    Guarantee an outcome

    You raise the odds. You don't fix the result. Check the answer before anyone walks out the gate on the strength of it.

    Replace a clear goal

    If you can't say what a good answer looks like, the model can't aim at it. Most bad prompts are unfinished thinking, not bad wording.

    Pocket checklist

    Before you hit send

    1. Could a sharp stranger who just arrived at camp produce exactly what I need from this?
    2. Have I said who it's for and what they'll do with it?
    3. Have I described the shape: length, format, structure?
    4. Would an example remove guesswork?
    5. Did I say what I want, rather than only what I don't?
    6. Is the source material in the prompt, clearly separated from my instructions?
    7. If I ran this ten times, would most of the answers keep people safe?
