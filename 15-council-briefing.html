Council Briefing

Every camp has more ideas than fuel. Scouts want radios, the kitchen wants a smoker, and someone has a plan to automate the gate. This manual helps the council decide where AI is worth the supplies. Today's AI is a strong drafter, summarizer and sorter of text that sometimes gets things wrong with total confidence. Spend on work where that trade pays off, measure it honestly, and keep people accountable.

Who it's for

Leaders and managers who decide which AI projects get time, money and people. No technical background needed.

Time

About 50 minutes, plus time with your own numbers.

Bring

Two or three candidate projects from your area, and a rough idea of how many hours they take today.

  • Start frequent, low-stakes and checkable.

    The best first projects happen often, involve lots of text, and produce output a person can check quickly.

  • Measure before you scale.

    Record a baseline, run a pilot against a comparison group, and decide in advance what result would make you stop.

  • People stay accountable.

    AI drafts and suggests. A named person owns each use and every decision that affects someone.

Part 1 · Know the recruit

What today's AI is good and bad at

Large language models write by predicting likely text, one piece at a time. That makes them fluent and fast with language. It also means they produce wrong answers in the same confident tone as right ones. Field Autopsy (Field Manual 0) explains why in about an hour, and it's the best single investment for a leader who wants to judge AI claims.

Reliable strengths
  • Drafting. First versions of emails, reports, job posts and plans. A person edits and sends.
  • Summarizing. Long documents, meeting notes and threads cut down to what matters.
  • Searching your documents. Answering questions from policies and manuals, with citations, when set up to retrieve from them (Archive Run, Field Manual 7).
  • Sorting and extracting. Routing requests by type, pulling fields out of forms, tagging feedback.
  • Coding help. Engineers write, review and explain code faster, with review still required (Squad Orders, Field Manual 5).
  • Rewording. Translating, simplifying, changing tone for a different audience.
Known weaknesses
  • Confident errors. Invented facts, citations or numbers that read as convincingly as real ones.
  • Inconsistency. The same question can get different answers on different runs or after small wording changes.
  • No memory unless built. Each conversation starts fresh unless the product stores and supplies history.
  • Knowledge cutoff. It knows nothing after its training date, or about your organization, unless you give it the information.
  • Shaky arithmetic and counting without a calculator or code tool attached.
  • Inherited bias. It reflects patterns in its training data, including unfair ones.

Part 2 · Choose the first missions

Score your candidate projects

Good first projects happen often, involve lots of text, tolerate the occasional error or are easy to check, have data available, can be measured, and have an owner who wants them. Avoid fully automated, high-stakes decisions early. Keep a person in the loop by default. Rate a project below, or load an example, then save it to compare.

    Part 3 · Requisition

    Buy, configure or build

    Most organizations use all three. Start with the cheapest option that fits the task, and move up only when you've measured a reason to.

    OptionWhat it isFits whenWatch for
    Buy an assistantA general chat assistant on an enterprise plan, available to staff for everyday drafting, summarizing and analysis.Many people, many small tasks, no integration neededUsage without measurable outcomes; data rules
    ConfigureAI features inside tools you already run (office suite, help desk, CRM), or no-code assistants set up with your instructions and documents.The work already lives in that toolPer-seat add-on costs; overlapping tools
    BuildEngineers connect a model to your data and systems for a specific workflow (Archive Run, Outpost Protocols, Supply Lines).High volume, a distinctive process, and off-the-shelf tools have fallen shortOngoing ownership, testing and running costs

    The costs that don't appear on the quote

    A license price is the smallest number in most AI budgets. Ask for each of these before you approve a project.

    EvaluationBuilding a test set from real cases and checking quality before launch and after every change (Proving Grounds, Field Manual 11).
    Review timeHours people spend checking AI output. It comes straight out of the time saved.
    IntegrationConnecting to your systems, permissions and single sign-on. Often bigger than the build itself.
    MaintenanceVendors update and retire models. Prompts, tests and integrations need re-checking each time.
    Change managementTraining, updated procedures, champions, and the time managers spend on adoption.
    GovernanceSecurity and legal review, policy upkeep, and keeping a register of AI uses.
    Usage growthPay-per-use pricing grows with success. Model the cost at full adoption.
    ExitGetting your data and configuration out if you switch vendors.
    Part 4 · Count the supplies honestly

    Measure value before you scale

    Estimates of time saved are usually guesses until you measure them, and self-reported savings tend to run high. A short, well-designed pilot settles the question cheaply.

    1. Record a baselineBefore anyone touches the tool, measure how long the task takes, how often, and how many errors it produces today.
    2. Pick outcome metricsTime per task, quality as judged by a reviewer, error rate and weekly active use. Decide which one matters most.
    3. Use a comparison groupA similar team or period without the tool. Without one, you can't separate the tool's effect from everything else that changed.
    4. Run long enoughSeveral weeks. The first days show novelty and learning-curve effects, and neither lasts.
    5. Check quality, not just speedFaster work that needs rework later isn't a saving. Have a reviewer score a sample from both groups without knowing which is which.
    6. Decide on pre-set termsWrite down what result means scale, adjust or stop before the pilot starts. Then follow it.
    Vanity metric
    Staff sent 5,000 prompts this month.
    Honest metric
    Median time to answer a supply request fell from 40 to 25 minutes in the pilot team. The comparison team stayed flat. Error rate on a reviewed sample was unchanged.
    Vanity metric
    We rolled out 300 licenses.
    Honest metric
    110 of 300 people used it in at least three of the last four weeks, mostly for report summaries and email drafts.
    Vanity metric
    92% of users say they like it.
    Honest metric
    Users estimate 3 hours saved a week. Timed samples show 1.5. We use 1.5 in the business case.

    Part 5 · Camp law

    Risk and governance

    Governance keeps the camp safe without stopping the work. Keep it proportional: a summary of a public report needs less control than a tool that shapes decisions about people. Select a topic.

    Which tools may see what

    A common pattern for data rules. Your security and legal teams set the real tiers and the approved tool list.

    Data classExamplesConsumer AI appsApproved enterprise tools
    PublicPublished reports, press releases, public web pagesAllowedAllowed
    InternalPlans, internal procedures, non-sensitive meeting notesNot allowedAllowed
    ConfidentialCustomer records, contracts, financials, unreleased productsNot allowedOnly tools approved for that data
    RestrictedHealth and personal data, credentials, classified or export-controlled materialNot allowedOnly specifically approved systems, often none
    Regulation is real and moving. The EU AI Act is risk-based: a few uses are banned, "high-risk" uses such as AI in hiring, credit or education carry heavy obligations, some systems such as chatbots must disclose that people are dealing with AI, and most other uses face little added regulation. Its bans and a duty to support staff AI literacy have applied since February 2025. A 2026 amendment moved most high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028. It can apply to organizations outside the EU whose AI is used there. Data protection, employment, consumer and sector laws apply to AI too. In the US, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a widely used voluntary guide. Ask your legal team what applies to you; this manual isn't legal advice.
    Part 6 · Traders at the gate

    Reading vendor claims

    Demos are rehearsed, benchmarks are chosen by the seller, and customer stories come from the happiest customers. None of that makes a vendor dishonest. It means you test on your own work before you sign.

    The perfect demo

    Every question answered flawlessly, on data the vendor prepared.

    Ask: can we bring our own examples, including messy ones and questions it shouldn't be able to answer? Then run a paid pilot on your data before a full contract.

    The accuracy number

    "95% accurate," with no detail.

    Ask: accurate on what data, judged by whom, compared to what baseline? What do the errors look like, and how would we catch them?

    The leaderboard

    Top score on a public benchmark.

    Ask: how closely does that benchmark resemble our task? Public benchmarks measure general ability, and models can be tuned to them. Your test set measures your work.

    The time-saved story

    "Customers save ten hours a week."

    Ask: measured how: timed against a baseline, or a survey? In what roles? Can we speak to a customer whose rollout went badly?

    "Your data is safe"

    Reassurance without specifics.

    Ask: what do you retain and for how long, do you train on our data, where is it processed, which subcontractors see it, and which security reports (such as SOC 2 Type II or ISO/IEC 27001) can we read? Get the answers in the contract.

    "Fully autonomous"

    No human needed.

    Ask: when it's wrong, how do we find out, who can override it, and what logs do we get? Autonomy without review is a risk you carry, not the vendor.

    Part 7 · The survivors

    People and change

    Tools change quickly. How people work changes slowly, and that's where most of the value is won or lost.

    Train by role

    Everyone gets the basics; builders get more. The course map has tracks: Foundations for everyone, Citizen builder for non-engineers who make their own tools, and App builder or Agent operator for engineers. Train on real tasks from people's own jobs.

    Involve the people who do the work

    They know where the hours go and which errors matter. Ask them to name the candidate projects, recruit volunteers as pilot champions, and let them shape the new process.

    Be straight about jobs

    Say what you know, what you don't, and what you've decided. Silence gets filled with worst-case rumors, and anxious people hide their best ideas.

    Redesign the workflow

    Adding AI to an unchanged process often moves the bottleneck instead of removing it. Ask which steps, approvals and handoffs still make sense now.

    Expect skills to shift

    More time goes to judging, checking and describing work clearly. Plan how newcomers will learn if AI handles the tasks they used to learn on.

    Make the right way the easy way

    If the approved tool is slow to get or weaker than a personal app, people will use the personal app. Approved tools, simple rules, fast access.

    Bolted on
    Analysts use AI to draft the weekly report faster. The same three managers still review it in sequence, and it still takes five days.
    Redesigned
    AI drafts the report from the logs on Friday morning with sources linked. One analyst checks the numbers against the sources. One manager approves. Two days, and the analysts spend the time they got back on follow-up.
